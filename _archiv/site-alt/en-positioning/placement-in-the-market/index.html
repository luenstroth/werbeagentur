Placement in the market: positioning in the competitive field

Location decides the price.

Positioning is not a self-description but a location in the competitive field — and that decides which price can be negotiated at all.

“Positioning” is usually understood as a self-image: what a company wants to stand for, which values it ascribes to itself, what character it intends for its brand. That is an internal matter and, as such, without consequence.

Here placement means something different — a location in the competitive field. Who are you actually compared against? On which shelf, in which tender, in which list of search results, in which procurement process? This location is an economic factor: it decides on listing, pricing latitude, sales effort — and on which arguments are heard at all.

The wrong placement is expensive, even if everything else is done right.

Five components of placement

1. Competitive distance

What is the brand confused with — and what does that confusion cost? Confusion is rarely an aesthetic problem and almost always a price problem: whoever stands next to a competitor that produces more cheaply is measured against that competitor's price. Distance is not created by claiming it, but by the brand being perceived in a different category.

2. Sales channel

Manufacturer, distributor and end customer assess the same offer by different criteria. Purchasing asks about margin, turnover rate and terms. Retail asks about shelf space and gaps in the range. The end customer asks about something else again — or does not ask at all, but simply reaches for it. A placement that serves only one of these stages gets stuck there.

3. Pricing latitude

The achievable price is not a result of the costing but of the position. A technically superior product that stands in the wrong frame of comparison is sold through discounts — and the sales force gets used to it. The correction does not start with the price list but with the question of what you are compared against.

4. Robustness in trademark law

A placement that cannot be defended legally is not a placement — it is a risk with an expiry date. This applies particularly to private labels that deliberately move close to a manufacturer brand, and to any naming meant to work internationally. How far you may go belongs in the design, not in a check afterwards: knowing the limit while designing saves revisions and gains latitude.

5. Findability

Today the competitive field includes search — and increasingly generative systems that reference, summarise and recommend brands without linking to them. A brand that does not appear in these answers does not appear for a growing share of research. That is not an SEO question but a question of how well a brand can be described in machine-readable form.

Evidence from our work

Albatros

The presence looked too much like a private label for the prices the product justified. The correction did not concern quality but the category in which the brand was read.

Kühlmann / Delikato

Retailer brand in the discount channel — discount price, but no cheap look. The placement had to serve two opposing expectations at once.

Cobiax

The advantage was real but understood by only part of the target group. A question of placement, not of product.

Volksbank PHD

After an acquisition: two brands, one architecture. The decision about the sender is a placement decision.

Securing it means not hoping

The difference between a positioning and a secured placement lies in testing. A position that is merely claimed lasts until the first counter-evidence — the first buyer meeting, the first shelf comparison, the first cease-and-desist letter.

Securing means anticipating each of these tests: the competitive comparison in the real environment, not on the mood board. Legal robustness before production, not after. The sales test before a range is rolled out. At Piasten, exactly this was the reason why some developments never made it into the range — the test took place, and it came out negative. That is not failure but the point of the exercise.
