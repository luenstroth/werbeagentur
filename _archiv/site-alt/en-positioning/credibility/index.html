Positioning · Yardstick

Credibility and persuasive power: the yardstick for everything else

Any machine can sound plausible these days. Being believed is something else.

There is a difference between a sentence that sounds right and a sentence that is believed. The first can now be produced in seconds — language models effortlessly generate phrasing that seems fluent, correct and appropriate. That is exactly why fluent phrasing is losing its value: it no longer proves anything.

Credibility arises another way. It is not a property of sentences but a consequence of decisions about content — and those cannot be automated, because someone has to stand by them.

What credibility consists of

Verifiability

A figure you can check carries more weight than any superlative. On the Delikato salad range, the fat content is printed on the pack variety by variety — from 4 % for tzatziki to 13 % for tuna salad. The range's promise is thus not claimed but quantified, including where the figure is less flattering. That is precisely what makes it credible: information that appears only when it is favourable is advertising. Information that always appears is a statement of fact.

Verifiable does not necessarily mean quantifiable, though. It is enough that the reader can check the claim against their own situation — at the very moment they read it.

A regional bank has no technical lead over direct banks — online banking is much the same everywhere. Its only advantage that cannot be copied is its proximity to the customer. The sentence “we are there for you locally”, however, appears in every other bank advertisement and is read accordingly: as a platitude.

So the advertisement does not claim proximity; it reveals it: an aerial photo of the town with every branch in it. Whoever opens the paper looks for their own street first — and answers the question of distance themselves before the bank has said anything about it. An argument the reader completes themselves does not have to be believed; they know it.

In passing, the advertisement turns the weaker position around. Not “we can do online too” — that would be an apology — but: everyone can do online, only here can you walk into a branch. The work lies in that decision, not in the design.

Consistency over time

A brand that claims something different every year has no statement, only a history of statements. Every change devalues the previous one — and because it usually comes with a new presence, it also devalues the recognition built up. That is the economic core of the principle change follows strategy: whoever changes rarely can rely on what they said before.

Omission

What you do not claim supports what you do claim. Most brand presences fail not from too little but from too much: every additional promise dilutes the others, and a single untenable one is enough to cast doubt on all the rest.

Omission is therefore not restraint but an investment — and it is the most uncomfortable part of the work, because someone in the company is attached to every point that is cut.

Persuasive power is not being liked

Being liked

Needs approval. Is measured in the approval committee: does everyone agree?

A draft that everyone agrees with has typically lost every edge on which a decision could have caught. That is no coincidence — edges are exactly where objections arise.

Persuasion

Needs a counterpart, a claim and a risk. Is measured in the market: does someone change their behaviour?

Persuasion presupposes that you could be wrong. Whoever risks nothing says nothing worth contradicting — and so nothing worth agreeing with either.

Three things follow operationally: a line of argument instead of a list — a thought that develops rather than a list of equally weighted points. One thought per carrier — an advertisement, a side of a pack, a slide carries exactly one statement. Dramaturgy instead of completeness — the order decides whether something lands, not the quantity.

The opposite of being liked is not sobriety

This point is regularly misunderstood, by agencies too: arguing against being liked is not arguing against feeling. In many markets emotion is not the decoration but the product — in confectionery, in professional sport, in fashion, in food. Communication that triggers no emotion there has failed its task, not fulfilled it.

The line runs elsewhere:

Borrowed emotion

It comes from the image, not from the subject: a sleeping child, a family in backlight, a sunrise. The feeling is real, but it belongs to no one.

The test: swap the sender. If the advertisement works equally well for a bank, an insurer and an energy supplier, the feeling was borrowed — and borrowed feelings do not pay into a brand.

Earned emotion

It arises from the facts themselves. The aerial photo above creates a feeling — recognition, reassurance, a small surprise — but only after the reader has found their own street.

This feeling cannot be transferred to another sender, because it is tied to the sender's concrete situation. That is exactly what makes it an asset rather than a mood.

Producing emotion has become cheap — a warm image and a warm sentence are available in minutes, in any quantity, from anyone. What has not become cheap: deciding which emotion carries with whom at the moment of decision, whether the product backs it up, and whether it still holds after the purchase. The decision about the emotion is the service, not its production.

What this says about the use of AI

In this work, artificial intelligence is a good tool and a poor yardstick. It speeds up research, variants, checks and production — and it is now the first line of defence when drafts are cleared, even before the law firm.

What it cannot do is decide what is left out. Omission requires accepting a loss that nobody demands — and taking responsibility for it. A system optimised to give a helpful answer will rarely conclude that the best measure is not to do something.
